P6: INTRODUCTION
P7: The development of digital technology has pushed higher education institutions to develop communication and marketing strategies capable of providing fast, relevant, and data-driven information to prospective students. Competition among universities has made information about study programs, service quality, and graduate prospects increasingly important in prospective students’ decision-making. Digital marketing through websites and social media has been found to influence these decisions, so digital channels no longer serve merely as promotional media but also as a means of providing information that supports institutional evaluation (Hermawan & Bimo, 2024). This situation indicates that universities need an information delivery mechanism that is not merely one-way, but interactive and relevant to users’ specific needs.
P8: One type of information with high potential to support these marketing needs is tracer study data. These data provide a comprehensive picture of graduates’ circumstances after completing their education, including employment status, waiting time, job-field alignment (linearity), and alumni career development. These indicators are crucial for describing graduate absorption into the labor market (Sadikin et al., 2023). Beyond serving as material for internal quality evaluation, this factual information has substantial potential to be communicated to prospective students to demonstrate the relevance of the education offered to industry needs. Thus, tracer study data can be transformed from a mere evaluation document into a strategic asset for institutional marketing.
P9: However, using tracer study data as an interactive information source still faces technical challenges in its management and presentation. Tracer study systems are generally still oriented toward presenting static data through forms, tables, or reports (Aprilia et al., 2022). Although web-based systems have improved data accessibility, users are still required to understand the interface navigation structure and filter the data manually. The problem becomes more complex when users, such as prospective students or parents, need specific answers that combine several indicators at once, such as waiting-time trends for a particular study program across several periods.
P10: The empirical dataset used in this study was officially obtained from the Tracer Study Unit and Career Center of Universitas Pembangunan Jaya (UPJ), covering three graduation periods (2022, 2023, and 2024) across 10 undergraduate study programs: Accounting (AKT), Management (MNJ), Psychology (PSI), Communication Studies (KOM), Product Design (DP), Visual Communication Design (DKV), Informatics (INF), Information Systems (SIF), Civil Engineering (TSP), and Architecture (ARS). The total graduate population was 1,655, with 1,292 valid traced respondents (average tracing rate of 78.07%, comprising 323 of 397 graduates [81.36%] in 2022; 476 of 595 graduates [80.00%] in 2023; and 493 of 663 graduates [74.36%] in 2024).
P11: The institutional variables managed include: graduates’ waiting time to obtain employment (<6 months, 6-18 months, >18 months), job-field alignment (a five-level scale from very close to not at all), and workplace scope (local/non-incorporated entrepreneurship, national/incorporated, and multinational/international). Based on a preliminary user-needs observation of 30 prospective students and parents, 86.7% of respondents reported difficulty obtaining graduate prospect information that combines several indicators at once (for example, the percentage of alumni working at multinational companies with a waiting time of less than 6 months), because the data are currently stored in separate, static, non-interactive annual accreditation report spreadsheets.
P12: Structured quantitative questions about graduate outcome summaries can be answered precisely and deterministically using standard SQL calculations. However, prospective students often ask relational questions that connect curriculum structure with career outcomes, such as the association between the elective course profile of a study program and the distribution of alumni across industry sectors. In the graph database schema developed here, this relationship is modeled through multi-hop graph path traversal ((:ProgramStudi)-[:OFFERS_COURSE]->(:Course) and (:ProgramStudi)-[:HAS_TRACER_STUDY]->(:TracerStudy), which is linked to (:CareerPath)). Because institutional tracer study data are aggregated at the study program level rather than tracking individual student grades, this cross-entity traversal functions as a graph-based relational association search (graph path traversal), not an inferential statistical correlation test. Therefore, the knowledge graph approach is positioned to complement the relational database in guiding the reasoning of Large Language Models (LLMs) on multivariable relational queries.
P13: The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) technology offers a potential solution to these challenges. RAG enables artificial intelligence models to synthesize answers based on specific external knowledge sources. However, conventional RAG based on vector search (semantic search) has limitations when faced with questions that require a global understanding of relationships between entities across multiple documents. As an alternative to the Microsoft GraphRAG approach (Edge et al., 2024), which emphasizes hierarchical text community summarization using the Leiden algorithm, this study designs a Knowledge-Graph-Grounded RAG (KG-grounded RAG) architecture optimized for structured institutional data in a Neo4j graph database. The graph schema is designed by modeling 16 node labels and 18 relationship types (edges) that represent the entities of the university, faculties, study programs, lecturers, courses, facilities, specializations, and annual tracer study indicators. The retrieval mechanism works in a directed manner by mapping the user’s query intent into a parameterized Cypher query. The relevant subgraph is then extracted and organized into a structured JSON evidence payload that is injected into the LLM prompt. This approach ensures that the language model’s generative answers remain strictly grounded in the university’s official facts and figures.
P14: Based on a review of prior literature, the development of intelligent assistants in higher education still leaves several research gaps. In terms of domain and data type, most current systems focus on administrative services using static text data, while the use of multivariable evaluation data such as tracer studies for campus marketing remains limited. In terms of retrieval method and relational support, existing systems generally rely on pure semantic search and have not explicitly utilized knowledge graphs to answer relational queries. In addition, few studies have integrated end-to-end workflows (such as n8n) or included an evaluation that compares system performance against conventional structured queries (SQL).
P15: Berangkat dari celah penelitian tersebut, penelitian ini bertujuan mengembangkan Asisten Informasi Kampus Cerdas berbasis graf pengetahuan di Universitas Pembangunan Jaya melalui arsitektur sistem yang memisahkan penanganan data ke dalam dua alur kerja (pipeline) independen. Pertama, Pipeline Ingesti dan Sinkronisasi Graf (Batch ETL) berjalan di latar belakang menggunakan skrip otomasi Python dan pemicu berkala untuk mengekstraksi data web admisi serta spreadsheet tracer study , melakukan validasi skema data, pembersihan batas privasi, deduplikasi simpul, hingga sinkronisasi atomik ke pangkalan data Neo4j menggunakan perintah Cypher MERGE. Kedua, Pipeline Inferensi Tanya-Jawab Daring (Online Inference) diorkestrasikan melalui alur kerja 7 node pada platform n8n yang bertugas menerima permintaan HTTP POST dari gateway web Flask (app.py), memetakan intensi masukan, mengeksekusi penarikan subgraf ke REST API Neo4j, menyusun muatan bukti terstruktur (structured JSON evidence payload), memanggil API LLM (GPT-4o-mini), dan mengembalikan respons terverifikasi kepada pengguna secara real-time.
P16: Based on these research gaps, this study aims to develop a knowledge-graph-based Intelligent Campus Information Assistant at Universitas Pembangunan Jaya through a system architecture that separates data handling into two independent pipelines. First, the Graph Ingestion and Synchronization Pipeline (Batch ETL) runs in the background using Python automation scripts and periodic triggers to extract admissions web data and tracer study spreadsheets, perform data schema validation, privacy-threshold sanitization, node deduplication, and atomic synchronization to the Neo4j database using the Cypher MERGE command. Second, the Online Question-Answering Inference Pipeline (Online Inference) is orchestrated through a 7-node workflow on the n8n platform, which receives HTTP POST requests from the Flask web gateway (app.py), maps the input intent, executes subgraph retrieval via the Neo4j REST API, assembles the structured JSON evidence payload, calls the LLM API (GPT-4o-mini), and returns a verified response to the user in real time.

P18: 1.1 Previous Research
P19: The closest prior work is that of Stepanov et al. (2026), who built a new-student admissions assistant using a three-layer architecture based on Neo4j. However, this study offers scientific novelty that differs significantly along four main dimensions. First, in terms of domain and data characteristics, Stepanov et al. (2026) focused only on static admissions documents such as selection schedules, administrative requirements, and test scores, whereas this study integrates three years of multivariable tracer study evaluation data (1,292 alumni) with the curriculum structure of study programs. Second, in terms of graph reasoning, the earlier study relied on three-layer intent routing (deterministic FAQ, Vector RAG, and Neo4j GraphRAG), whereas this study applies multi-hop graph traversal to connect course entities, lecturer profiles, and study program career outcomes, along with direct numerical aggregation. Third, in terms of evaluation protocol, Stepanov et al. (2026) measured annotated factual accuracy on general admissions flows, whereas this study applies separate evaluation schemes covering Exact Numeric Match against an SQL oracle, multi-hop reasoning tests, and privacy-protection evaluation using k-anonymity (k ≥ 5). Fourth, in terms of workflow orchestration, the custom monolithic Python backend service approach of the earlier study is replaced by a modular architecture based on a low-code workflow engine (n8n) acting as an API orchestrator integrated with the Flask Web Gateway.

P21: RESEARCH METHOD
P22: 2.1 Research Flow
P23: This study was conducted at Universitas Pembangunan Jaya (UPJ) using official recapitulated tracer study data for the 2022 to 2024 graduation periods, covering 10 study programs, together with the institution’s academic marketing information data. The research procedure was organized systematically into seven main stages, as shown in Figure [=].
P24: 
P25: The explanation regarding each stage of the research shown in Figure 1 is outlined as follows:
P26: Identifikasi Masalah 
P27: The initial stage of the study identified problems in the management and presentation of tracer study data at UPJ, which remain static. This condition makes it difficult for prospective students and parents who need interactive information that combines several graduate outcome indicators and academic information at the same time. In addition, differences in the characteristics of user questions were identified, which fall into structured numerical aggregation queries, cross-source relational queries, and open-ended natural-language questions.
P28: Problem Formulation
P29: Based on the results of the problem identification, the research focus was formulated on how to design and build an intelligent campus marketing assistant that integrates the GraphRAG architecture with the n8n workflow orchestrator. This stage also set a testing boundary: the benefit of the knowledge graph approach in enriching Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning is treated as a hypothesis to be tested empirically, while deterministic numerical aggregation queries continue to rely on the computational reliability of the relational database (SQL).
P30: Requirements Analysis
P31: The requirements analysis stage covered an inventory of data sources, software specifications, and the establishment of information security and alumni data privacy rules. The data collected consisted of tracer study indicators (employment status, job-search waiting time, job-field linearity, and industry distribution) and public academic information (tuition fees, scholarship pathways, lecturer profiles, curriculum, and facilities). Because alumni data are sensitive, de-identification rules were applied by removing all individual identities and restricting access to public aggregate data only. A minimum respondent-group threshold was also applied to prevent the risk of identity disclosure in small data groups.
P32: Application Design
P33: The design stage focused on graph database modeling and the integration architecture between system components. Institutional data and graduate outcome summaries were mapped into a knowledge graph schema in the Neo4j database, in which entities (study programs, courses, alumni career indicators, and facilities) are represented as nodes connected by semantic relationships (edges). Architecturally, the system was designed to be modular, dividing roles between the Flask framework as the web service managing the user conversation interface and the n8n platform as the behind-the-scenes data processing orchestrator.
P34: Application Development
P35: The development stage was the implementation and coding of a virtual assistant, which connects the Flask web application, the Neo4j database, and the LLM service through an automated workflow in n8n. The n8n processing flow was implemented through seven nodes operating sequentially:
P36: Webhook Chat (n8n-nodes-base.webhook): Serves as a REST API-based entry point that handles inbound HTTP POST requests containing the user’s text message through the /chat endpoint.
P37: Build Cypher Query (n8n-nodes-base.code): Functions as a JavaScript-based preprocessing unit (rule-based text parser/router) that analyzes input keywords (such as tuition fees, scholarships, lecturers, curriculum, facilities, and tracer study), filters out queries that violate individual privacy, and then dynamically constructs the graph query (Cypher query).
P38: Query Neo4j REST API (n8n-nodes-base.httpRequest): Acts as the data communication link between n8n and the Neo4j graph database through HTTP POST transactions to execute Cypher queries and extract the relevant subgraph of entities and relationships.
P39: Format Prompt (n8n-nodes-base.code): Functions as the Prompt Augmentation component that assembles the Neo4j extraction results into a structured JSON context, then combines it with the user’s question and the system instructions (prompt template).
P40: LLM (n8n-nodes-base.httpRequest): The artificial intelligence inference component that sends the prompt to the GPT-4o-mini model via API with a temperature of 0.4 and a token limit, so that the virtual assistant persona produces source-grounded answers that can be verified.
P41: Extract LLM Response (n8n-nodes-base.code): Handles post-processing by extracting the answer text from the LLM’s JSON response and providing error mitigation (a fallback response) in the event of workflow failure, missing data, or API problems.
P42: Kirim ke Web (n8n-nodes-base.respondToWebhook): Serves as the workflow terminator that returns the response payload in JSON format synchronously to the Flask web interface.
P43: Discussion
P44: This stage covers system testing and a comprehensive analysis of the evaluation results. Testing used 50 to 60 initial test questions grouped into six scenarios: single-fact questions, numerical aggregation, cross-entity comparison, multi-entity relational reasoning, ambiguous questions, and scenarios with no available data or outside the scope (out-of-scope). System answers were evaluated against reference answers (ground truth) validated using verified SQL queries for numerical metrics and official campus documents. Subsequently, GraphRAG performance was compared with a conventional RAG baseline on an equivalent dataset based on the parameters of factual accuracy, numerical accuracy, source attribution, handling of information failures, and response time.
P45: Conclusion Drawing
P46: The final stage of the study is drawing conclusions based on the empirical findings from the testing and discussion, in order to answer the research objective regarding the feasibility of the n8n-integrated GraphRAG architecture as an intelligent campus marketing assistant.
P47: 
P48: RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
P49: 3.1 Implementation of an n8n-Based GraphRAG Integration Workflow
P50: The implementation of the intelligent marketing assistant is realized through workflow orchestration on the n8n platform, which synchronously connects the web interface, the Neo4j graph database, and the LLM inference service. Data processing begins when a user query is received through the Webhook Chat (/chat), which forwards the HTTP POST payload to the Build Cypher Query node. In this node, a rule-based text parser maps the user’s intent and keywords (such as tuition fees, lecturer profiles, curriculum, facilities, and graduate career distribution) into a dynamic Cypher query.
P51: The Cypher query is then executed by the Query Neo4j REST API node through an HTTP POST transaction to the Neo4j database to extract the relevant subgraph. The retrieved entity and relationship data are reformatted in the Format Prompt node into structured JSON as augmentation context, which is then combined with the system prompt and the assistant persona. A low temperature setting (0.2-0.4) does not by itself guarantee deterministic factual correctness, so answer factuality is ensured through a dual-verification mechanism. First, a Structured Evidence Payload is applied, in which the context from graph retrieval is packaged as an explicit JSON object containing verified data attributes before being inserted into the language model prompt. Second, a negative constraint in the system prompt explicitly instructs the model to produce a standard fallback answer when the data in the graph context is null or empty, and prohibits the model from extrapolating numbers beyond that graph context. As reinforcement, the temperature is set to 0.1 specifically for number and fact extraction tasks to suppress the stochastic variation of the LLM, which was verified empirically through testing on the test dataset. After the answer text is extracted and validated in the Extract LLM Response node, including a fallback handling mechanism for API transmission failures, the response is returned to the user interface in real time through the Kirim ke Web node.
P52: 
P53: 3.2 System Evaluation Design and Procedures
P54: To empirically test the reliability of the system, an evaluation dataset was constructed consisting of 60 test questions grouped into six information-need clusters:
P55: Single Fact Questions (10 items): Tests the retrieval of simple static information about study programs, scholarships, and campus facilities.
P56: Numerical Aggregation Queries (10 items): Tests quantitative calculations based on tracer study data, such as the percentage of employed graduates and the average graduate waiting time.
P57: Cross-Entity Comparison Queries (10 items): Tests comparative analysis of outcome profiles or characteristics between two or more study programs.
P58: Multi Entity Relational Reasoning (10 items): Tests the traversal of indirect relationships across entities (for example, the link between the curriculum competency profile and the absorption of alumni into industry sectors).
P59: Ambiguous and Incomplete Queries (10 items): Tests the system’s ability to detect query ambiguity without producing hallucinated facts.
P60: Failure and No-Data-Availability Scenarios (10 items): Tests the system’s response to out-of-scope input, personal alumni privacy questions, and the absence of data in the knowledge base.
